Welcome!

Welcome to Ethics! In this course we will be delving into core issues about values, rules, and how we should live life. When (if ever) are sacrifices permissible or even mandatory? What sorts of moral consideration do other people/animals/environments deserve? Is there a true, objective morality, or is it all subjective/relative to culture? What constraints should there be on speech, capital punishment, abortion, euthanasia, AI, or immigration (just to name a few)? In this class, we will learn about major approaches to these questions and more. More importantly, we will develop the skills to analyze and evaluate these approaches.

Premier quantum physicist & philosopher Sean Carroll loves to point out that physics is way easier than ethics. Areas like physics are clean, clear, and under control. Ethics, on the other hand, is blurry, difficult, and extremely hard to do well. In a (sufficiently described) complicated physical system, the answer is nearly always clear. In ethics, we’ll see that that simply isn’t the case. We have to work harder. Humans comprise the most complicated systems in the (known) universe. It is no wonder that figuring out the right ways for us to live is challenging.

In this class, we explore some of the biggest questions in ethics and hone the skills that are required to answer them. We start with some of the most classic questions: whether morality is objective, do rules or results matter more, etc. before moving to more complicated approaches later on.

This is a condensed section. We cover one topic per week, and each week’s work is due at the end of that week. Because there is no slack built into the schedule, falling a week behind is difficult to recover from.

The number one tip for success in this class: ENGAGE — do the readings, watch the videos, discuss with classmates, stay up to date with D2L, and above all reach out if you run into trouble.

Human Proof - The first assignment is an unfortunate necessity today, but gives us a chance to have some fun. Given the rise of fraudulent (LLM backed) students, it’s important that we can weed out anyone here that doesn’t have a beating heart. For any of us to get something out of this class, we need to be able to trust we’re actually having legitimate conversations and we’re not merely wasting our time with bots. For that reason, the first assignment requires that you prove you are a human. To be clear, this assignment is ridiculously easy, if you are in fact a human. Details are in D2L. This assignment is not graded, but it is required: it must be completed before any other assignment, and it is how you establish attendance in this course.

Participation - The participation grade is earned by actively engaging in the weekly discussion boards. These activities include the following: discussions/debates, peer writing evaluation workshops, group critical analysis sessions, and others. These are largely completion based. They are meant to be low stress ways for you to engage with others and develop your skills. (There are certain philosophical skills that are simply impossible to develop without engaging with others.) There may be situations where I’d like to discuss your posts further in a video call. There may even be situations where I’d prefer not to grade the post until we discuss it further.

Quizzes - There are quizzes for every topic we cover, all of which are on D2L. These are due each week, alongside the discussion for that week. This is meant to keep you engaged with the material as we move through the course. If you wait till the deadline and some issue arises, you will very likely not get a mass extension on all of the quizzes.

These are simple multiple choice/multi-select questions asking about the course material. These should not take you too long if you are engaging with the material. But, if you do have trouble, don’t hesitate to reach out. You have multiple attempts on each quiz specifically so that you can come ask questions when you need help finding an answer.

Final Paper - The final paper is where you demonstrate the skills you’ve developed on the question you chose. It is not graded just on the final product, but on how you meet milestones and build on feedback through an iterative process. You will be required to start halfway through the semester, and there will be two peer review discussions (one on outlines and another on the paper itself) to help you develop your ideas and skills.

To steer clear of funny business, you will be required to write the paper from start to finish inside the provided writing environment, PersonifyAI.app.

Late work policies

I grant at most one extension for an assignment, and generally speaking I do not grant mass extensions. That means if you miss submitting several weekly quizzes, then I will likely only grant an extension on one. Peer review discussions must be completed during their assigned week to receive credit, and they cannot be made up after the fact.

Each extension is contingent on being professional. As college students, you should recognize yourselves as professional students. (Anything less, and you’re not fully appreciating the level of investment you’re making.) That means I expect you to communicate timely, clearly, and respectfully. In addition, it means you have realistic standards for yourself and your instructors. I will work with you on extensions so long as you are professional in your requests and expectations. Here’s an example of a bad email:

“hey I missed Thursday three weeks ago”

Can you spot all of the errors? The recipient isn’t addressed. There is no signature. It is not clear which class or section the student is in. The first letter isn’t capitalized, and there’s no punctuation. These are all issues that might seem small but show a lack of attention to detail and likewise a lack of respect for the recipient. Worst of all, there is no actual question. It is just a statement, meaning it is asking the recipient (i.e. the instructor) to figure out what you need and what you’re looking for. Finally, this email is way too late. If you (expect to) miss a deadline, you need to handle it as soon as possible. Here is a better approach:

“Dear Dr. Conte, I hope this finds you well. I am in your PHI-1012-70L class. You may have noticed I was unable to submit my finished paper by the deadline on Friday (Nov 13). If possible, I would sincerely appreciate an extension to submit the assignment for full or partial credit until the following Monday (Nov 16). I understand that it is my responsibility to complete assignments by the due date and there is no guarantee that I get an extension. Thank you for your consideration and have a great day! Billy Pilgrim S0123456789”

Plagiarism and Artificial Intelligence (AI)

Plagiarism. If you are having difficulty with a paper or MLA citations, please communicate with me. I’ll be glad to work with you. Plagiarism, in my view, is something students resort to when they feel desperate. If you are behind, not sure you understand the assignment, are struggling with the assignment, don’t understand how the MLA citation system works, or a similar situation, please get in touch with me. We will work one-on-one to get you back on track. This is a much better solution than turning in work which is not yours. Many students don’t always realize that in academic communities, plagiarism is a serious offense, and is grounds for failing an assignment or course and/or disciplinary action. Plagiarism means copying passages directly from the text of any source, without quotation marks and citations, or not citing summarized or paraphrased information. The written work you submit must be original: written entirely by you alone and not previously submitted in another class. All quotations, summaries and paraphrases (restatements of others’ ideas) need to be cited within your paper. No credit will be given for plagiarized papers and/or assignments. Students who plagiarize will be referred to the Provost.

Artificial Intelligence (AI).

Imagine asking someone, “Hey, I’m hungry. Could you go eat lunch for me?”. You’re not asking if they’ll bring you lunch. You’re asking that they will eat lunch and in doing so satisfy your hunger. Absurd, right? Someone else eating doesn’t do anything for your empty stomach.

Now imagine doing the same for learning. “Hey, I’d like to learn how to swim. Could you learn how for me?” If the only thing that happens is your friend learns how to swim, you should still steer clear of the deep end.

There are some things that cannot be done for you. Learning is one of these things. Your use of external resources -whatever they are- should always be done in service of your learning, helping you master the material and skills better.

Completing assignments with regurgitated LLM responses will not lead to you learning (nor passing).

Standard Competencies/Outcomes

  1. Explain major ethical concepts and describe the problems and/or issues in clarifying them that are used to comprehend and express moral or ethical problems.
  2. Analyze major ethical theories.
  3. Explain each major ethical theory from the perspective of a follower to identify what is significant in a particular situation or context for that theory.
  4. Justify how each ethical theory reaches conclusions about what should be done regarding specific moral problems.
  5. Reflect on the logical implications and consequences of the conclusions that each ethical theory reaches.
  6. Demonstrate philosophical and/or logical thinking through writing and other forms of assessment.
